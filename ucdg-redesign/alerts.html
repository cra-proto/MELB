Alerts

An alert is a short, temporary notice at the top of a page or section. It tells the user something has changed, gone wrong, or needs their attention right now — before they keep going with their task.

Choose the right pattern

Before you write an alert, decide whether an alert is what the user actually needs.

  • Use an alert

    Use when the situation is short, urgent, and page-specific.

    Best for: service outages, submission errors, confirmation of a completed action, time-sensitive deadlines, a single change the user needs to see once.

  • Use a central information page

    Use when the change is large, ongoing, or affects many pages.

    Best for: a theme-wide disruption, a multi-week rollout, a change with rules and dates that keep evolving. Link to the central page from the affected topic pages.

Quick decision guide

  • Will this notice still be relevant in a month? → Central page
  • Does it need more than 2 sentences to explain? → Central page
  • Does it apply to only one page or task flow? → Alert
  • Is the user's current task blocked or affected right now? → Alert

Use the correct alert

Once you have decided an alert is right, choose the type that matches the situation. The four types are colour-coded and carry different meanings.

Info alert (blue)

Use the info alert to draw attention to a clarification of surrounding content or helpful advice, such as the number of weeks it will take to receive a refund when an application is sent by mail vs by phone.

You can also use it for the length of time an application method takes, changes in wait times on a phone line, messages that there is no service disruption for a particular service if that is generating inquiries.

Success alert (green)
Use success alert to draw attention to a successful action, such as to confirm a submission, the completion of a task, or when a service disruption is resolved.
Warning alert (yellow)

Use the warning alert to draw attention to a possible consequence of an action or inaction, such as legal penalty that could apply.

You can also use it to warn the user about delays, closures at certain locations, and other types of service disruptions.

Danger alert (red)

Use the danger alert to draw attention to a situation that could place someone in danger, such as a do not travel warning, or if there's a risk to health or safety in completing an activity.

You can also use it to alert a person of a technical issue that could cause them to be unable to complete their task, such as an invalid submission of data in a form, when systems are down or a service is cancelled.

What to include

  • A descriptive title that names the specific situation (not "Note" or "Important")
  • One or two sentences describing the impact on the user
  • What the user should do, if there is an action they can take
  • At most one link, only if the user needs more detail to act

Guidance

  • Write the title so a reader can decide whether to keep reading based on the title alone
  • Lead with the impact on the user, not the internal reason for the change
  • Use plain, second-person language: "You may see a delay" — not "Users may experience latency"
  • Do not use an alert to add emphasis to normal content. If it is not urgent, it is not an alert
  • Do not use an alert to announce that something is new — use a label instead
  • If the alert has more than one link, split it or move it to a central page

Recommended titles

Titles pulled from real CRA situations. Copy the pattern, not the exact wording.

  • Service disruption
  • Payment delayed
  • Application received
  • Deadline approaching
  • We need more information
  • Action required to keep your benefit

Placement

  • Whole site: at the very top of every page. Reserve for site-wide outages.
  • Whole page: immediately below the H1, above the intro paragraph.
  • Section-specific: directly above the section it applies to, not somewhere else on the page.

Coding considerations:

If the alert doesn't have a title, add invisible text inside a <span class="wb-inv"> to provide the same experience to screen reader users
  • Begin <section> with a heading that is specific to the featured content
  • Do not use <section> within another section, or validation issues occur

Accessibility

  • Colour is never the only signal. Every alert type also has an icon and a text label.
  • Use role="alert" for time-sensitive messages so screen readers announce them immediately.
  • Do not put an alert inside a collapsible or hidden container — it must be visible on page load.
  • Icons carry aria-hidden="true"; the meaning is in the visible label, not the icon.

Examples

Compare alerts side by side

Good alert examples
  • Payment delayed. Your July payment will arrive 3 business days late because of a processing error. No action is needed. Payments will resume on schedule in August.
  • Application received. We received your Canada Dental Benefit application on July 14, 2025. You will hear back within 6 weeks.
  • We need more information. We cannot process your return until we receive your T4 slip. Upload the missing document.
  • Deadline approaching. The last day to file your 2024 T1 return without a late-filing penalty is April 30, 2025.
Alerts to avoid
  • Important. The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) has recently implemented several updates to the systems that support the processing of individual income tax and benefit returns for the 2024 tax year, which may result in variations to standard processing timelines. Users are encouraged to consult the appropriate documentation for further information regarding these updates, including but not limited to the following: link 1, link 2, link 3.
    Why it is wrong: vague title, jargon, no impact statement, multiple links, way too long — this belongs on a central page.
  • Note. New feature available.
    Why it is wrong: generic title, no context, no action, and "new" is not urgent — use a label, not an alert.
  • Warning. Latency may be experienced.
    Why it is wrong: passive voice, jargon ("latency"), no impact on the user, no timeframe, no next step.
  • Update. Please be advised that as per policy XYZ, the processing timeframes have been amended effective immediately.
    Why it is wrong: "please be advised" and "as per" are bureaucratese; "amended" hides the actual change; no timeframe named.
