And / Or

A visual pattern that puts the word "and" or "or" between the items in a list of criteria so the reader cannot misread the combinator. Use it wherever the wrong reading would send someone through the wrong door — eligibility, required documents, or any rule that combines several conditions.

Choose the right pattern

Reach for And / Or whenever a bulleted list of rules could be misread. If the reader would guess wrong, the pattern belongs on the page.

  • Use the "And" list

    When the user must meet every item. Missing one item means they do not qualify. Common in "Before you start" and eligibility.

  • Use the "Or" list

    When meeting any one item is enough. Common in "You can do this online, by mail, or by phone" and in accepted documents.

Quick decision guide

  • Do all items need to be true for the rule to hold? → And
  • Does the user pick or provide one item from a set? → Or
  • Is it a mix (must have A, and either B or C)? → Split into two nested lists, each with its own combinator
  • Is the list a plain sequence with no combinator (steps in order)? → Use a numbered list, not And / Or

What the pattern looks like

The design puts the word "and" or "or" between blocks so the relationship is unmistakable at a glance. Use "and" when every item is required; use "or" when any one is enough.

"And" — the user needs every item:

  • Be a resident of Canada for tax purposes

  • Have filed your most recent tax return

  • Have a child under 18 in your care

"Or" — the user needs any one item:

  • A utility bill from the last 90 days

  • A bank statement from the last 90 days

  • A lease signed within the last 12 months

What to include

  • A short introductory line naming what the list is (for example, "To qualify, you must meet all of the following:")
  • The word "and" or "or" visibly rendered between items — not only in the intro line
  • Concise items, one criterion each, in the order that most users will encounter
  • A visible signal (a colour band, a label, or a repeated word) so scanning the list alone cannot mislead

Guidance

  • Never rely on the reader inferring the combinator from context. Say it and show it.
  • Every item is a full, standalone statement — not a fragment that only makes sense after reading the intro line.
  • Do not mix "and" and "or" in a single flat list. Nest them or split them.
  • Keep lists short. If you have more than 6 items, consider whether some of them are one item ("proof of residence" instead of five bullets for the accepted documents).
  • Do not use bold or italics to signal the combinator. Use the visible And / Or pattern so the meaning survives on paper, in screen readers, and when styles fail.

Recommended phrasing

  • To qualify, you must meet all of the following
  • You need all of these documents
  • You can submit in any of these ways
  • Show us one of the following

Accessibility

  • The word "and" or "or" appears in the visible text, not only as an icon or a colour.
  • Screen readers read the combinator as part of each item (or between items), not as a separate decorative element.
  • Colour cues are backed by shape or text — a coloured left border alone is not enough.
  • Nested combinators use real semantic nesting (a list inside a list), not indentation alone.

Examples

Compare combinator lists side by side

Good And / Or examples
  • To qualify, you must meet all of the following:
    • be a resident of Canada for tax purposes
    • and have filed your most recent tax return
    • and have a child under 18 in your care
    Why it works: the combinator is stated in the intro and shown between every item; the reader cannot misread the list as an "or."
  • You can prove your address with any one of these documents:
    • a utility bill dated in the last 90 days
    • or a bank statement dated in the last 90 days
    • or a lease agreement signed within the last 12 months
    Why it works: "any one" in the intro plus "or" between items makes the choice unmistakable.
  • You need proof of identity and proof of income:
    • Proof of identity (any one of): passport or driver's licence or permanent resident card
    • and Proof of income (any one of): T4 slip or most recent pay stub or notice of assessment
    Why it works: nested combinators split cleanly, each with its own scope.
And / Or lists to avoid
  • You are eligible if:
    • you are a resident of Canada
    • you have filed your most recent tax return
    • you have a child under 18
    Why it is wrong: the reader must guess whether these are all required or any one. Most will assume "or" and skip the tax return.
  • Bring your SIN and a photo ID or a utility bill.
    Why it is wrong: ambiguous — is it (SIN and photo ID) or utility bill, or SIN and (photo ID or utility bill)? A visible list with combinators would settle it.
  • Requirements:
    • Canadian resident
    • 18+
    • SIN
    • employed
    • filed 2023 return
    • direct deposit
    Why it is wrong: fragments, no combinator, no clue what "requirements" means in the aggregate. Rewrite each as a full sentence and add "and" between them.
