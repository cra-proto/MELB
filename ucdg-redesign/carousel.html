Carousel

A carousel cycles through slides of content in one spot. It looks efficient, but research is consistent: readers miss everything past the first slide, and moving content is hard to use with a keyboard or screen reader. On task-focused CRA pages, the honest default is don't. Stack the content instead, so every reader can see it.

Choose the right pattern

Almost always, the content in a carousel reads better stacked down the page. Reach for a carousel only in the rare case where the items are equal, optional, and genuinely benefit from one frame.

  • Stacked content

    The default. Put each item in the flow of the page so every reader sees all of it. Almost always the right answer.

  • One prominent item

    If only one thing matters most, feature it — don't rotate it out of view behind others.

  • Carousel

    A last resort, for a few equal, optional items. Expect most readers to see only the first.

Quick decision guide

  • Does the reader need to see all the items? → Stack them
  • Is one item the priority? → Feature that one
  • Are the items equal, optional, and few? → a carousel may be acceptable, reluctantly

If you must use one

  • Visible, large previous/next controls and slide indicators — never rely on swipe alone
  • No auto-advance, or an obvious pause control, so content doesn't move out from under the reader
  • A small number of slides, each understandable on its own
  • The most important message never hidden inside the carousel

Guidance

  • Prefer stacking. If the content matters, don't hide it on a slide the reader may never reach.
  • Never auto-rotate without a pause control — moving content is a barrier and a distraction.
  • Don't put essential information (deadlines, eligibility, the main task) in a carousel.
  • Keep slides few and equal; a carousel is not a place to bury a long list.

Accessibility

  • Controls must be real buttons, reachable and operable by keyboard, with clear labels.
  • Auto-advancing content must be pausable, and must not move faster than a reader can follow (a WCAG requirement).
  • The current slide and total count must be announced to assistive technology.
  • Don't trap keyboard focus inside the carousel.

Examples

Compare approaches side by side

Good approach
  • Three benefit highlights stacked down a landing page, each a short section with a link, so every reader sees all three.
  • A single, prominent "Sign in to My Account" feature instead of a rotating banner.
To avoid
  • An auto-rotating banner carousel with the filing deadline on slide three.
    Why it is wrong: most readers never reach slide three, and auto-rotation is a barrier. Put the deadline in the page.
  • A carousel navigable only by swiping, with no visible buttons.
    Why it is wrong: keyboard and many touch users can't operate it. Provide real controls.
