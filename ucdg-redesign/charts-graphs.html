Charts and graphs

A chart turns numbers into a shape the reader can grasp at a glance — a trend over time, a share of a whole, a comparison between groups. It earns its place only when the pattern matters more than the exact figures. When the reader needs the numbers themselves, a table is better. And a chart is never enough on its own: the same data must be available as text.

Choose the right pattern

Charts, tables, and text present numbers differently. Pick by whether the reader wants the pattern, the exact values, or a single takeaway.

Chart or graph

Use when the pattern is the point — a trend, a comparison, a proportion the reader should see at a glance.

Table

Use when the reader needs the exact values, or will look up a specific figure.

Plain text

Use when there's a single takeaway — "Filings rose 12% from 2023 to 2024" — that a sentence states more clearly than a chart.

Quick decision guide

  • Is the trend or comparison the point? → Chart (with a data table behind it)
  • Will the reader look up exact figures? → Table
  • Is it one number or one change? → Plain text

What to include

  • A title that states what the chart shows and the takeaway, not just "Figure 1"
  • Labelled axes and a clear legend, with units named
  • The underlying data as an accessible table, so the figures are available to everyone
  • A short text summary of the pattern the chart reveals

Guidance

  • Only chart data when the shape matters. A chart of three numbers is usually a sentence.
  • Match the chart type to the data: lines for trends over time, bars for comparisons, parts-of-a-whole sparingly.
  • Don't distort the picture — start axes at a sensible baseline and keep scales honest.
  • Keep it simple. A cluttered chart with many series is harder to read than a table.
  • Always pair the chart with the data in text — a chart alone is not accessible.

Accessibility

  • Provide the chart's data as a real data table so screen-reader users get the same information.
  • Never use colour alone to distinguish series — add labels, patterns, or direct annotation.
  • Ensure colours meet contrast, and check the chart is legible in greyscale.
  • Give the chart a text alternative summarizing its main point; don't leave it as an unlabelled image.

Examples

Compare charts side by side

Good use of charts
  • A line chart of on-time filing rates over five years, titled with the takeaway, with a data table below and a one-line summary of the trend.
  • A bar chart comparing processing times by channel, with each bar directly labelled and colours that also read in greyscale.
Charts to avoid
  • A pie chart of two values that a sentence would state more clearly.
    Why it is wrong: two numbers don't need a chart. Write "62% online, 38% by mail".
  • A chart image with no data table and no text summary.
    Why it is wrong: it's invisible to screen readers. Always provide the data as text.
  • A bar chart whose axis starts at 90 to exaggerate a small difference.
    Why it is wrong: it distorts the picture. Start the axis at a sensible baseline.
