Large checkboxes and radio buttons

Checkboxes and radio buttons let a reader make a choice. The large GCWeb style gives each option a big, tappable target — easier to select, especially on a phone. The one rule people get wrong: use radio buttons when only one answer is allowed, and checkboxes when more than one is.

Choose the right pattern

The choice is simple but decisive: how many answers can the reader pick?

Radio buttons

Use when the reader may pick only one option from a set — a filing status, a single year, a yes/no.

Checkboxes

Use when the reader may pick any number — none, one, or several — such as selecting the documents they have.

Quick decision guide

  • Only one answer allowed? → Radio buttons
  • More than one answer allowed? → Checkboxes
  • A single on/off choice? → one checkbox (for example, "I agree")
  • Many mutually exclusive options that won't fit? → consider a select menu, but prefer radios when the list is short

What to include

  • A clear question or label for the group, so the reader knows what they're choosing
  • Options that don't overlap, each with a plain, distinct label
  • A large, tappable target — the whole option, not just the small control
  • A visible selected state that doesn't depend on colour alone

Guidance

  • Match the control to the rule: radios for "pick one", checkboxes for "pick any". Getting this wrong confuses and misleads.
  • Keep option labels short, parallel, and non-overlapping (see Interactive questions for overlap pitfalls).
  • Make the whole option clickable, not just the tiny box or circle — that's the point of the large style.
  • Don't pre-select an option that commits the reader to something (like consent) — let them choose.
  • Group related options under one clear question; don't scatter them.

Accessibility

  • Use real <input> controls with associated <label>s, grouped in a <fieldset> with a <legend>.
  • Every control must be reachable and operable by keyboard, with a visible focus state.
  • The selected state must be conveyed by the control itself, not by colour alone.
  • The large tap target helps people with motor and dexterity differences — keep it large on every screen.

Examples

Compare checkboxes and radio buttons side by side

Good use
  • "Which tax year are you filing for?" with radio buttons — only one year can apply.
  • "Which documents do you have?" with checkboxes — the reader may have several, each a large tappable row.
To avoid
  • Checkboxes for "Select your filing status", where only one status can be true.
    Why it is wrong: checkboxes imply multiple answers. Use radio buttons for a single choice.
  • Radio buttons for "Which benefits are you receiving?", where several can apply.
    Why it is wrong: radios allow only one. Use checkboxes when more than one answer is possible.
  • A tiny checkbox with the label far to the side, hard to tap on a phone.
    Why it is wrong: it defeats the large-target style. Make the whole option tappable.
