Headings and titles

A heading is the short, bold line that names the content right after it. The page title is the H1 at the very top; the headings below it (H2, H3, H4) break the page into sections. Most people scan headings for keywords rather than reading top to bottom — so headings are how a reader, and a screen reader, decide they are in the right place.

Choose the right pattern

Almost every page needs a single, descriptive H1. Use a stacked title only when the page belongs to a larger set and its own title is not enough to place it.

Single descriptive H1

The default. Use whenever you can write one clear, unique title that makes sense on its own — for example, Tax information for students.

Topic stacked H1

Use when a task belongs to a larger topic and could be confused with the same task under another topic — Personal income tax / After you file your tax return. Coded as one H1.

Subway stacked H1

Use only on pages that use the subway pattern — Canada Dental Benefit / Get ready to apply. The design carries two H1s.

Quick decision guide

  • Can you write one clear title that makes sense on its own? → Single H1
  • Would that title collide with the same task under a different topic? → Topic stacked H1
  • Is the page a step inside a subway product? → Subway stacked H1

What to include

  • One page title (H1) that describes, in plain language, exactly what the page is about
  • A <title> tag that matches the H1, with "- Canada.ca" added to the end
  • Section headings (H2 and below) that divide the content roughly every 200 words
  • A first paragraph that introduces the page in plain language — it often becomes the search-result snippet

Guidance

Writing the heading

  • Make it short and clear. No unnecessary words, no promotional claims. Only use an abbreviation if the audience knows it better than the long form — otherwise pair them, like "Registered Education Savings Plans (RESPs)".
  • Front-load keywords. Put the words people search for first — they scan the left edge and rarely read on. Prefer "Tax rates and brackets for individuals" over "Individual tax rates and brackets".
  • Use sentence case. Capitalize only the first word and any proper nouns. (Capitalize each word only in the titles of guides, forms, and reports.)
  • No end punctuation. Don't end a heading with a period or a question mark. "Who is eligible" — not "Who is eligible?"
  • Follow a heading with text, not another heading — except where the page purpose is obvious, or the next heading introduces an "On this page" list.

Choosing the page title (H1)

  • Match the H1 to the <title> tag. They should read the same. For a stacked title, include both parts in the title tag: <title>How to apply - Disability tax credit (DTC) - Canada.ca</title>.
  • Make it unique. Search "your title site:canada.ca" — if other pages share it, add context so a reader can tell yours apart in a results list.
  • Keep it short. Search results cut off around 60 to 65 characters, so lead with the words that matter.
  • Update inbound links when you rename a page so existing links still describe where they lead.

Organizing with headings (H2 and below)

  • Create a scent of information. Titles, headings, links, and the first paragraph are what a scanning reader follows toward their task — write them to point the way.
  • Use parallel structure. Sibling headings should match grammatically — for example, all gerunds ("Writing…", "Choosing…", "Organizing…").
  • Nest by level, never by size. Go H1 → H2 → H3 in order. Don't skip a level, and don't pick a heading level for how big it looks.

Recommended phrasing

Rewrites that apply the rules above. Copy the move, not the exact words.

  • Unique and descriptive: "Managing injury and illness case file notes – Template" — not "File notes"
  • Most important words first: "Information Management Policy" — not "Policy on Information Management"
  • No unnecessary words: "Recruitment program" — not "Recruitment program information – What you need to know"
  • Concise: "Approving travel requests" — not "How to approve travel requests"

Accessibility

  • Use exactly one H1 per page — the page title.
  • Do not skip heading levels. Screen-reader users navigate by heading level; a jump from H2 to H4 hides structure.
  • Use real heading elements, not bold text styled to look like a heading. Assistive technology only recognises true headings.
  • Keep the H1 and the <title> tag in sync so the browser tab and the on-page title agree.

Good heading and title examples
  • Tax information for students — descriptive and unique. A reader arriving from search knows the page is about them, not a generic "Students".
  • A stacked topic title: Corporate income tax / After you file your tax return — tells the reader which "After you file" section this is.
  • Parallel H3s under "Writing good headings": Make them short and clear, Front-load keywords, Follow capitalization rules — all phrased as instructions.
Headings and titles to avoid
  • Page title: Students.
    Why it is wrong: not unique or descriptive in a results list. "Tax information for students" tells the reader what the page actually offers.
  • Heading: Are you eligible?
    Why it is wrong: we provide answers, not questions, and headings don't end in punctuation. Use "Who can apply".
  • A section that goes H2 "How to apply" straight to H4 "Documents you need".
    Why it is wrong: skipping H3 hides the structure from screen-reader users navigating by level.
  • Bold, larger paragraph text used in place of a real heading.
    Why it is wrong: assistive technology can't find it and can't jump to it. Use a true heading element.
