Labels

A label is a short, text-based marker that calls attention to a status or category — "New", "Closed", "Beta". It's a single word or two attached to something, telling the reader what kind of thing it is. A label is not a count and not a message, so keep it to a word and let it mean something.

Choose the right pattern

Labels sit between badges and alerts. Pick by what the reader needs: a category, a count, or a message.

Label

A short word marking a status or category — "New", "Archived", "Closed".

Badge

A count of items — three new messages, two tasks remaining.

Alert

A temporary message about something that changed or needs attention now.

Quick decision guide

  • Marking what kind of thing it is? → Label
  • Showing how many? → Badge
  • Telling the reader something urgent? → Alert
  • Announcing something is "new"? → a label, not an alert

What to include

  • One or two words that name the status or category plainly
  • A consistent set of labels across the product, so the same word always means the same thing
  • A clear anchor — the item the label describes

Guidance

  • Keep labels to a word or two. If it needs a sentence, it's an alert, not a label.
  • Use a small, consistent vocabulary. Don't mix "Closed", "Ended", and "Done" for the same state.
  • Don't overuse labels — if everything is labelled, the labels stop meaning anything.
  • Use a label (not an alert) to mark something as new; "new" is a status, not an urgent message.

Accessibility

  • The label's meaning must be in its text, not its colour — never signal a status by colour alone.
  • Ensure the label text meets contrast against its background.
  • Make the relationship between the label and its item clear to a screen reader, not just visually adjacent.

Examples

Compare labels side by side

Good use of labels
  • A "New" label beside a recently added service in a list, so returning users can spot what changed.
  • "Closed" beside a consultation that has ended, using the same word everywhere consultations close.
Labels to avoid
  • A "label" that reads "Please note that this section has recently been updated with new information".
    Why it is wrong: that's a sentence — an alert, not a label. A label is a word or two.
  • A status shown only by a coloured dot with no word.
    Why it is wrong: colour alone fails for many readers. Put the status in text.
  • Every item on a page tagged with a coloured label.
    Why it is wrong: when everything is labelled, nothing stands out. Reserve labels for what matters.
