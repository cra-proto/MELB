Links

A link sends the reader to more content. The link text is a promise about where they will land — so the words you underline matter more than almost anything else on the page. Good link text tells a scanning reader, and a screen-reader user jumping link to link, exactly what they will get.

Choose the right pattern

Before you style something as a link, decide what the reader is actually doing: going somewhere, or performing an action.

  • Text link

    Use to send the reader to another page or a spot further down the page. The default. Underlined, in the flow of content.

  • Link styled as a button

    Use for the single primary action of a page — "Apply now", "Sign in to My Account". One per page; buttons lose meaning in a crowd.

  • Rescue link

    Use directly below the page title to catch readers who landed on the wrong page — a short link to the page they probably wanted.

Quick decision guide

  • Sending the reader to more information? → Text link
  • The one main action of the page? → Button (only one)
  • Many readers arrive here by mistake? → Rescue link under the title
  • A pile of related pages at the end? → a grouped "Related information" list of links

What to include

  • Link text that names the destination — the reader should know where it goes without the surrounding sentence
  • Links that work — check every one, and re-check after a page is renamed or moved
  • Sentence-case link text, matching the title of the page it points to
  • A note when a link behaves unexpectedly — opens in a new tab, downloads a file, or leaves Canada.ca to content in one language only

Guidance

  • Make the link text represent the destination. "Canada Dental Benefit eligibility" — not "click here" or "read more". A screen-reader user hears the link out of context.
  • Avoid links mid-sentence. A link buried in a sentence is easy to miss and breaks the reading flow. Prefer a link at the end, or on its own line.
  • Open links in the same tab by default. Only open a new tab when leaving the current task would lose the reader's work — and say so.
  • Don't put links in italics. Italic underlined text is hard to read and easy to mistake.
  • Email addresses: link the actual address as its own text (name@cra-arc.gc.ca), not hidden behind "email us".
  • Vanity URLs: use a short, memorable, plain-language address for print or spoken use (for example, canada.ca/taxes). Request one through the proper CRA process; don't invent your own.
  • Linking outside government: when the destination is external and not available in both official languages, flag it so bilingual users know what to expect.

Placement

  • In content: at the end of the relevant sentence or on its own line, not buried in the middle.
  • Rescue links: immediately below the H1, before the intro, so a misdirected reader escapes quickly.
  • Related information: grouped at the end of the page under a clear heading, as a list — not scattered through the body.

Accessibility

  • Link text must make sense on its own — screen-reader users navigate by pulling up a list of links stripped of context.
  • Never use "click here", "here", or a bare URL as the link text.
  • Keep links underlined in the content area (the design system default) so they're distinguishable without relying on colour.
  • If a link opens a new tab or downloads a file, tell the user in the link text — an unexpected context change is disorienting, and it's an Accessible Canada Act expectation.
  • Don't use the same link text for two different destinations, or different text for the same destination.

Examples

Compare links side by side

Good links
  • "Check your eligibility for the Canada Workers Benefit." — the link text names exactly where it goes.
  • A single button, "Sign in to My Account", as the one primary action at the top of a service page.
  • A rescue link under the title of a corporate page: "Looking for personal income tax? Go to Personal income tax."
Links to avoid
  • "For information on filing, click here."
    Why it is wrong: "click here" tells a screen-reader user nothing when read out of context, and it's not scannable.
  • Five buttons in a row: Apply, Learn more, Contact, FAQ, Home.
    Why it is wrong: when everything is a button, nothing is the primary action. Use one button and make the rest text links.
  • A link mid-sentence that opens a new tab with no warning.
    Why it is wrong: it's easy to miss, and an unannounced new tab disorients the reader. Move it and flag the new tab.
  • "Email us" linking to a hidden address, and the same text "More" used for three different pages.
    Why it is wrong: show the real email address, and give each destination unique, descriptive link text.
