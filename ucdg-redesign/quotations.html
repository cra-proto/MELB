Quotations

A quotation marks words that came from someone other than the page's author — a taxpayer, a piece of legislation, a report. Use the right markup so the source is clear and the quote is announced as a quote, and keep quotes short and purposeful.

On this page

  • What to include
  • Guidance
  • Accessibility
  • Examples

What to include

  • A short quotation set inline with the surrounding sentence, using inline quote markup (<q>) which supplies the quotation marks.
  • A longer quotation set off as a block quote (<blockquote>), visually separated from the body.
  • Attribution — who said or wrote it, and where it comes from, so the reader can trust and trace it.

Guidance

  • Only quote when the exact words matter. If you are paraphrasing, write it as plain text, not a quotation.
  • Keep quotes short. Quote the sentence that matters, not three paragraphs of legislation — link to the source for the full text.
  • Always attribute the quote. An unattributed quotation raises more questions than it answers.
  • Don't use block quotes as a decorative "pull quote" to enlarge ordinary text — that's emphasis, not quotation.

Accessibility

  • Use real quotation markup (<q> or <blockquote>) so assistive technology can announce the boundaries of the quote.
  • Put the attribution in text next to the quote, not conveyed by styling alone.
  • Don't rely on indentation or a colour bar as the only signal that text is a quotation.

Good quotations
  • A short, attributed block quote from the Income Tax Act, with a link to the full section for the exact legal text.
  • A one-line taxpayer comment in a research summary, quoted inline and attributed to "a participant in usability testing".
Quotations to avoid
  • A three-paragraph block quote of a regulation dropped into the page.
    Why it is wrong: too long to read in place. Quote the key sentence and link to the source.
  • An ordinary sentence set as a big block quote to make it stand out.
    Why it is wrong: that's emphasis, not a quotation. Use bold, or a heading, instead.
  • A quote with no source given.
    Why it is wrong: an unattributed quote can't be trusted or verified. Always attribute.
