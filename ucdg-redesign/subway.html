Subway pattern

The subway pattern turns a long, complex product into a series of related tasks, broken into steps, with a persistent menu that follows the reader through the journey. Use it to help people understand a product, check whether they qualify, and apply — the way the Canada Dental Benefit or the Disability tax credit does.

Choose the right pattern

The subway pattern is powerful and easy to misuse. Only reach for it to guide a reader through a series of related tasks. If the content isn't a task journey, one of the simpler options is correct.

  • Single page

    Use when the whole task fits on one page a reader can scroll. Add an "On this page" list if it gets long. Most content belongs here.

  • Subway pattern

    Use when a product is too big for one page and moves the reader through a sequence — understand, check eligibility, apply, follow up.

  • Sub-pages

    Add within a subway only when a single step is too complex or varied for one step page — never as a way to bolt on unrelated content.

Quick decision guide

  • Is this a series of related tasks around one product or service? → Subway pattern
  • Does the whole task fit on one scrollable page? → Single page
  • Is one subway step too complex or varied for a single page? → Sub-page within that step
  • Are the pages unrelated, or grouped only for convenience? → Not a subway — use a topic or index page

What to include

The subway pattern is a set of page types, each with its own role. A subway has at least two levels of information architecture and can grow to three.

  • Index page (required). The entry point. Introduces the product and holds the subway menu of steps.
  • Step pages (required). One page per task in the journey. Each carries the persistent subway menu showing where the reader is.
  • Sub-pages (optional). A third level for a step that is too complex for one page — either connected (part of the step's flow) or non-connected (referenced from it).

Order the steps along the reader's "happy path" — basic details first, then forward through the process:

  1. Introduce the product or service — a short statement of what it is, on the index page. If it's complex, use a first step page titled "What is the [product]".
  2. Explain who it's for — eligibility. Prefer plain wording: "Who can apply", "Who can claim", "Who can file".
  3. Explain the core activity — the calculating, applying, claiming, registering, or filing the reader came to do.
  4. Explain any follow-up activities — what happens after they act.
  5. Provide contact details (optional) — where to get help if they can't self-serve.

Guidance

  • Only use the subway pattern to guide a reader through related tasks. Misusing it creates inconsistency across Canada.ca and breaks the information-architecture, visual, and interaction-design principles the pattern depends on.
  • Follow the happy path. Lead with the basics and move forward in a logical order — don't make the reader jump around the menu to assemble the task.
  • Use the recommended wording for step titles ("Who can apply", "How much you can get") so subway products read consistently.
  • Keep each step to a single clear task. If a step needs more than one page, that's a signal for a sub-page, not a longer step.
  • Don't nest endlessly. Three levels of IA is the ceiling — beyond that, rethink the structure.

Placement

The subway pattern is mobile-first: the code defaults to the mobile design and transforms for desktop. Step and sub-pages carry two H1s, positioned differently by screen size.

  • On mobile: the subway menu sits at the top of the page, directly below the breadcrumbs, wrapped in a border that includes the first H1. The "Sections" label is hidden.
  • On desktop: the subway menu moves to its own column on the right of the content, with the "Sections" label above it and no wrapping border. The first H1 appears as smaller grey text directly above the main H1.

Accessibility

  • The persistent menu is real navigation — mark the reader's current step with aria-current="page" so screen readers announce where they are.
  • Step and sub-pages legitimately use two H1s in this pattern; keep the smaller "product" H1 first and the step H1 second, in the DOM order the design specifies.
  • The menu must be reachable and operable by keyboard, and its order must match the reading order when it reflows from the desktop column to the top on mobile.
  • Don't rely on the subway line or colour alone to show progress — the current step must be clear from text and markup too.

Examples

Compare subway uses side by side

Good subway examples
  • Canada Dental Benefit. Index page introduces the benefit; steps run Who can apply → How much you can get → Get ready to apply → Apply → After you apply, following the reader's happy path.
  • Disability tax credit (DTC). The product is complex, so the index page gives a high-level description and the first step page is "What is the DTC" before eligibility and applying.
  • A single "Apply" step is too varied for one page, so it uses a connected sub-page for "Apply online" and another for "Apply by mail" — still inside the same subway.
Subway uses to avoid
  • A subway groups five unrelated CRA topics — Payroll, GST/HST, Charities, Appeals, Careers — under one menu because they share an audience.
    Why it is wrong: these aren't a series of related tasks. The subway implies a sequence that doesn't exist. Use a topic or index page.
  • A two-paragraph "How to update your address" task is split into a four-step subway.
    Why it is wrong: the content fits on one page. A subway adds navigation overhead with no benefit.
  • Steps are ordered Apply → Who can apply → What it is.
    Why it is wrong: it reverses the happy path. Introduce the product and eligibility before the core activity.
  • A step page nests sub-pages four levels deep to fit an edge-case scenario.
    Why it is wrong: the subway supports three levels of IA. Deeper than that, the structure needs rethinking, not another level.
