Tabbed interface

A tabbed interface separates related content into panes, showing one at a time. It suits content the reader chooses between — different variants of the same thing — where they rarely need to compare across panes. If the reader must read everything, or compare, tabs hide content and get in the way; stack it instead.

Choose the right pattern

Tabs trade visibility for tidiness. Only make that trade when the reader picks one pane and ignores the rest.

  • Tabbed interface

    Use for parallel variants the reader chooses between — "Online" vs "By mail" — and rarely compares side by side.

  • Stacked sections

    Use when the reader needs to read or compare everything — put each part in the flow with headings.

  • Interactive question

    Use when a single answer should collapse the page to just the relevant branch.

Quick decision guide

  • Does the reader pick one variant and ignore the rest? → Tabbed interface
  • Do they need to read or compare all of it? → Stacked sections
  • Should one answer reveal just the relevant content? → Interactive question

What to include

  • Clear tab labels that name each pane's content, not "Tab 1"
  • A small number of tabs — a long row of tabs is a menu in disguise
  • Panes that each make sense on their own
  • A sensible default tab selected on load

Guidance

  • Only use tabs when the reader chooses one pane. If they need everything, stacking is clearer.
  • Don't hide content the reader must compare across tabs — comparison across hidden panes is exactly what tabs prevent.
  • Keep the number of tabs small and the labels short and distinct.
  • On a phone, make sure the tabs reflow or collapse gracefully rather than overflowing.

Accessibility

  • Use the proper tab pattern (role="tab"/role="tabpanel") so assistive technology announces the tabs and which is selected.
  • Tabs must be operable by keyboard, with a visible focus state and arrow-key navigation between them.
  • Content in hidden panes must still be reachable when its tab is selected; don't remove it from the accessibility tree.
  • Don't signal the selected tab by colour alone.

Examples

Compare tabbed interfaces side by side

Good use of tabs
  • "How to pay" with tabs for "Online", "By mail", and "In person" — the reader picks their channel and reads only that.
  • Rate tables for "2024" and "2023" as two tabs, where a reader wants one year at a time.
Tabs to avoid
  • Application steps split across tabs the reader must visit in order.
    Why it is wrong: sequential steps shouldn't be hidden in tabs. Stack them, or use the subway pattern.
  • Eligibility criteria the reader must compare, split across two tabs.
    Why it is wrong: tabs prevent side-by-side comparison. Stack the content.
  • A row of ten tabs used as page navigation.
    Why it is wrong: that's a menu, not a tab set. Use a list of links or a hub page.
