Text formatting

Text formatting is how you emphasize a word or phrase — bold, italic, alignment, size, colour. Used sparingly, it draws the eye to what matters. Used everywhere, it draws the eye to nothing. The rule underneath all of it: formatting is never the only thing that carries meaning.

Choose the right emphasis

Pick the lightest tool that does the job. If several words on a page are emphasized, none of them stand out — so reserve emphasis for the rare word that truly needs it.

  • Bold

    Use to make one key word or short phrase stand out in a sentence — a deadline, an amount, a "do not". Strongest emphasis; use it least.

  • Italic

    Use for titles of publications and the rare term being defined. Not for emphasis — italics are harder to read in long runs.

  • A structural element

    Often the real answer. If you're formatting to organize, use a heading, a list, or an alert instead.

Quick decision guide

  • Do you want to organize content? → Use a heading, list, or alert, not formatting
  • Do you need one word to stand out in a sentence? → Bold, once
  • Is it a publication title or a defined term? → Italic
  • Never use underline for emphasis — underline means "link"

What to include

  • Emphasis only where a scanning reader genuinely needs it — usually none to one per paragraph
  • Left-aligned text for all running content (English reads left to right)
  • The design system's default text size — set size with the correct heading level, not by resizing body text
  • Colour that also carries a non-colour cue (an icon, a label, or bold) whenever it signals meaning

Guidance

  • Bold, italic, underline: bold for a single key word; italic for titles and defined terms; never underline anything that is not a link.
  • Alignment: keep body text left-aligned. Do not centre or justify paragraphs — centring is hard to scan and justification creates uneven spacing.
  • Sizing: do not enlarge or shrink text to add emphasis. Size belongs to heading level; changing it breaks the hierarchy.
  • Colour: do not colour text for decoration, and never use colour as the only way to tell one thing from another. Keep enough contrast against the background.
  • Wrapping and truncating: let text wrap; do not truncate content the reader needs. Only truncate where the full value is available another way (for example, a long reference shown in full on its own page).

Accessibility

  • Never rely on colour, bold, or italic alone to convey meaning — pair it with a word, icon, or structure a screen reader can announce.
  • Text colour must meet WCAG contrast against its background (at least 4.5:1 for body text).
  • Do not underline non-link text; underline is a universal signal for links and will mislead every reader.
  • Avoid justified text — the uneven "rivers" of space make it harder to read, especially for people with dyslexia.

Good text formatting
  • "You must file by April 30, 2025 to avoid a late-filing penalty." — one bold phrase marks the single fact the reader cannot miss.
  • A publication is named in italics: "See the Employers' Guide – Payroll Deductions for the current rates."
  • A status is shown with an icon and a word, not colour alone: a green check beside "Received" and a red X beside "Missing".
Text formatting to avoid
  • A paragraph with five bolded phrases to "highlight the important parts."
    Why it is wrong: when everything is bold, nothing is. Emphasis only works when it's rare.
  • The word "important" is underlined for emphasis.
    Why it is wrong: underline means "link". Readers will try to click it. Use bold instead.
  • Eligibility statuses shown only as red or green text.
    Why it is wrong: colour alone fails for colour-blind users and screen readers. Add an icon or label.
  • A block of instructions centred on the page.
    Why it is wrong: centred body text has no steady left edge to scan down. Keep it left-aligned.
