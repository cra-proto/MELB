Table of contents

A table of contents is a short list of links that lets a reader jump straight to the part of a page they need — without scrolling to hunt for it. It does the same job across pages when it acts as a directory of the subpages inside a product.

Choose the right pattern

There are two kinds of table of contents, and one case where you should use neither. Decide based on where the content the reader wants actually lives.

  • "On this page" list

    Links to headings further down the same page.

    Best for: a long single page divided into sections, where a reader would otherwise scroll two or three times to find what they need.

  • Index-page directory

    Links out to other pages within a product.

    Best for: a landing page whose only job is to route the reader to the right subpage.

  • No table of contents

    Skip it entirely.

    Best for: short pages the reader can see without scrolling, and pages that are mostly tables or images with no headings to link to.

Quick decision guide

  • Does the reader scroll more than two or three times to see everything? → "On this page" list
  • Is the whole page just a menu to other pages? → Index-page directory
  • Does the page fit on one or two screens, or has no real headings? → No table of contents
  • Does the content live across several web pages? → Not an "On this page" list — link out instead

What to include

  • A heading that labels the list: "On this page" for same-page links, "Table of contents" for an index directory
  • Links presented as a list — bullets for an unordered set, numbers when the order matters
  • Mainly the H2 headings on the page; add a few H3s only when they carry real user value
  • Standard link styling, so every item plainly looks clickable

Guidance

  • Keep the list no more than three levels deep. If you need more, the page is too complex for one page — split it.
  • Present the list vertically, aligned left. Do not lay a same-page "On this page" list out in columns.
  • For an index directory, columns are fine when link titles look crowded — but keep multi-column lists to a single level, and group nested links into grids so children stay tied to their parent.
  • Match the link text to the heading it points to, word for word. A reader should recognise where they landed.
  • Do not include the H1, the "On this page" heading itself, or the footer sections in the list.

Placement

  • Place an "On this page" list below the H1 and the page's short introduction, but before the substantive content begins.
  • An alert or a key image may sit above the table of contents when it must be seen first — but keep the list high on the page.
  • An index-page directory is the content of its page: it sits directly under the H1 and intro, with nothing competing below it.

Accessibility

  • Every anchor link must point to a real heading ID on the page, so keyboard and screen-reader users land on the section, not near it.
  • Use genuine list markup (<ul> / <ol>) so assistive technology announces the number of items.
  • The list is a navigation aid, not a substitute for headings — the sections it points to must still have proper H2/H3 headings.
  • Do not rely on colour alone to mark the links as clickable; keep the standard underlined link style.

Examples

Compare tables of contents side by side

Good table-of-contents examples
  • A long "Who can apply for the Canada Dental Benefit" page opens with an On this page list of its four H2 sections — Who can apply, How much you can get, How to apply, After you apply — each linking down to its heading.
  • A "Taxes" landing page uses a Table of contents directory in two columns to route the reader to Personal income tax, Business taxes, GST/HST, and Payroll subpages.
  • A short "How to change your address" page with two headings has no table of contents — the reader can see the whole page at once.
Tables of contents to avoid
  • A two-paragraph page adds an "On this page" list with a single link.
    Why it is wrong: nothing to navigate. A table of contents on a page you can see at a glance is just noise.
  • An "On this page" list links to headings that live on three different web pages.
    Why it is wrong: an "On this page" list must link within the same page. Cross-page links belong in an index directory.
  • A table of contents nests four levels deep, down to individual H5 subheadings.
    Why it is wrong: past three levels the list is harder to scan than the page. Cut to H2s, or split the page.
  • The link text reads "Section 2" while the heading it points to reads "How much you can get."
    Why it is wrong: the reader cannot tell where the link goes, and the mismatch breaks the scent of information.
