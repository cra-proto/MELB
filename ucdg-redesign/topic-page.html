Topic page

A topic page is a routing page. Its job is to send a reader onward to the sub-topics and related pages that help them complete a task — not to explain the task itself. It is built mostly from doormats and a "Most requested" block, and it succeeds when a reader can find their next step in seconds and leave.

Choose the right pattern

A topic page routes; it doesn't hold the task. Pick it when the reader's next move is to choose a sub-topic, not to read or do something here.

Topic page

Use to route readers to the right sub-topic or related page across a subject area — mostly doormats and most-requested links.

Subway pattern

Use when the reader follows a sequence of tasks for one product — understand, check, apply, follow up.

Content page

Use when the reader's task is here — the page explains or lets them do the thing, not routes them elsewhere.

Quick decision guide

  • Is the reader here to choose a sub-topic? → Topic page
  • Are they stepping through one product's tasks? → Subway pattern
  • Is the task on this page itself? → Content page

What to include

  • A clear H1 naming the subject area, and a short intro that says what the reader can do from here
  • A "Most requested" block for the handful of top tasks, placed first
  • Doormats grouped into related sets, each a titled, described link to a sub-topic
  • A "Contact us" route for readers who can't self-serve (see Contact us)

Guidance

  • Keep it a router. Don't turn a topic page into a long read — link out to the pages that hold the detail.
  • Lead with "Most requested" for the top tasks, then the fuller set of doormats. Don't repeat the same links in both.
  • Group doormats into meaningful sets with headings, ordered by importance to the reader.
  • Follow the doormat rules for titles and descriptions so the sets read as a cohesive whole.
  • Don't decorate the links with icons or images for emphasis.

Accessibility

  • Use real headings to label each group of links so screen-reader users can navigate between sets.
  • Every link title must make sense on its own, out of context in a list of links.
  • Don't rely on layout columns or colour alone to group links — use headings and structure.
  • Keep link titles unique across the page so no two read identically.

Examples

Compare topic pages side by side

Good topic pages
  • A "Taxes" topic page opens with "Most requested" (file a return, make a payment, sign in), then grouped doormats for Personal, Business, and GST/HST, each routing to a sub-topic.
  • A "Benefits" topic page with clearly grouped, described doormats and a contact route at the end — the reader finds their benefit and leaves quickly.
Topic pages to avoid
  • A topic page that explains the whole filing process in full before any links.
    Why it is wrong: a topic page routes; it shouldn't hold the task. Move the detail to a content page and link to it.
  • The same links repeated in "Most requested" and again in the doormats below.
    Why it is wrong: duplication clutters the page. Don't repeat most-requested links in the doormats.
  • Fifty ungrouped links in one long list.
    Why it is wrong: an undifferentiated wall of links is hard to scan. Group them with headings, ordered by importance.
